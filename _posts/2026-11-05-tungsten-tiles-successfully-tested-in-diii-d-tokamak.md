Researchers from North Carolina State University, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and JEOL have successfully tested 3D-printed tungsten tiles inside the DIII-D tokamak under conditions simulating the intense heat and particle bombardment of plasma-facing components in future fusion reactors. Tungsten remains the leading candidate material for tokamak walls because of its high melting point, thermal conductivity, and ability to withstand extreme irradiation. However, its ductile-to-brittle transition temperature and high melting point make conventional manufacturing of complex geometries difficult. The team's additive manufacturing approach overcomes these constraints, enabling production of intricate shapes optimized for heat transfer and plasma confinement. Successful demonstration of 3D-printed tungsten components is a key step toward enabling commercially viable fusion reactor designs, as manufacturing flexibility could reduce both cost and time-to-deployment for next-generation fusion systems.
