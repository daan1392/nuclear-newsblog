A team at General Atomics led by Jeff Lestz demonstrated helicon current drive in a nuclear fusion tokamak experiment, using radio waves to sustain electric current inside the confined plasma. The technique involves steering radio waves around the toroidal (donut-shaped) reactor geometry so they are absorbed by the plasma, inducing the necessary current to maintain confinement.

Magnetic confinement fusion reactors require continuous current in the plasma to generate the magnetic fields that hold it stable at extreme temperatures. Traditional tokamaks used brief inductive pulses, limiting operation to seconds or minutes. External current drive methods like helicon waves could enable sustained or pulsed operation without these constraints.

The clear demonstration of helicon current drive leverages latest advances in tokamak diagnostics, plasma heating, and radio frequency systems. If helicon or competing current drive methods mature, they could enable reactor designs that operate continuously rather than in repeating pulses, improving efficiency and thermal power extraction. The achievement advances tokamak physics and may support the next generation of demonstration reactors being developed internationally.
